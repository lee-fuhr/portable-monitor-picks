How to choose a portable monitor

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Focus on your laptop’s USB-C video output support first. Then choose a size and brightness that match how you’ll actually use it, indoor office light needs less than outdoor work. IPS panels are the safe, affordable choice; OLED is for media enthusiasts who don’t mind the premium.

The one-cable USB-C experience: what it is and why it matters

The big promise of modern portable monitors is a single USB-C cable carrying both video signal and power to the screen. When it works, you just plug in and go, no separate power brick, no HDMI adapter, no second cable. That convenience is the main reason most people buy a portable monitor. But here’s the catch: not every USB-C port on your laptop is wired to output video. Some are data-only, some are charging-only. You need a port that supports “DisplayPort Alt Mode” (often marked with a little monitor icon or “DP” next to the port). Thunderbolt 3 or 4 ports also work. If your laptop has neither, you’ll need an HDMI-to-USB-C adapter and a separate power source for the monitor, which kills the single-cable dream. Check your laptop’s specs online or look for the icon. If you’re buying for a work machine, this step alone can save you a lot of frustration.

Size and weight: how much screen do you really need?

Portable monitors generally come in three size clusters: 13- to 14-inch, 15- to 16-inch, and the rare 17-inch. The 15- to 16-inch models are the sweet spot for most people, enough room to have two documents side by side without squinting, yet still light enough to slip into a laptop bag. A typical 15.6-inch panel weighs around 1.5 to 2 pounds; a 13-inch model shaves off a few ounces but also cuts screen area noticeably. Step up to a 17-inch monitor and you gain even more real estate, but you also get a significantly bigger footprint. Those models often need a larger bag and can feel awkward on a cramped café table. Ask yourself honestly: will I be moving this thing between desks daily, or mostly staying put at one or two locations? Frequent travelers should lean toward the lighter 13- or 15-inch options. Weight matters, but thickness and bezel design also affect how easily the monitor fits in your bag. Some slim models are barely thicker than a smartphone, while others with integrated kickstands add bulk. Look for user reviews that mention actual bag-fit experience.

Brightness, resolution, and panel type: what actually looks good?

Brightness is the number one spec to pay attention to, especially if you ever work near a window or outside. A typical indoor portable monitor offers 250 to 300 nits, which is fine in an office with controlled lighting. But if you plan to use it on a sunny patio, you’ll want 400 nits or more. Many budget models fall short here, so read owner feedback about outdoor visibility. Resolution is a more personal choice. At 15 inches, 1080p is perfectly sharp for most productivity tasks, text is crisp enough at normal viewing distance. 1440p looks noticeably finer, especially for photo editing or reading lots of code, but it also requires more graphics power and tends to cost more. 4K at 15 inches is overkill for most people; you’d only appreciate it for high-end photo or video work, and it will drain your laptop’s battery faster. Panel type: IPS is the standard for good reason, wide viewing angles and solid color accuracy at a reasonable price. OLED panels offer stunning contrast and deeper blacks, ideal for watching movies or creative work where color fidelity matters. But OLED costs significantly more, and some users worry about burn-in or higher power consumption. For 99% of portable monitor buyers, a well-made IPS panel is the right call.

Stand design and build: integrated kickstand vs. cover-stand vs. no stand

The stand can make or break daily use. There are three main approaches: an integrated kickstand built into the monitor’s back, a folio-style cover that folds into a stand (like a tablet case), or no stand at all (you have to prop the monitor against something or buy a separate stand). Integrated kickstands are the most reliable, they tend to be sturdy, offer adjustable tilt, and don’t get lost. But they add thickness and weight. Folio cover-stands are clever because they also protect the screen when closed, but the folding angles can be limited and they sometimes slide on smooth desks. No-stand models are the lightest but force you to find a solution (a small book, a phone stand, etc.), which quickly becomes annoying. Also look at the tilt range. Some stands only tilt backward, not forward, which matters if you set the monitor below eye level. And check whether the stand feels stable when you touch the screen, if it wobbles, you’ll hate using it. User photos and reviews are your best source for this.

Extra features: refresh rate, touch screens, and power delivery

For most productivity work, 60 Hz is plenty. Higher refresh rates (like 120 Hz or 144 Hz) are real benefits only if you plan to play fast-paced games. They add cost and can also increase battery drain, so don’t pay extra unless you’re a gamer. Touch screens are convenient for scrolling or marking up documents, but they add cost and often make the screen surface more reflective. If you mainly type and use a mouse, skip touch. If you regularly annotate or swipe through presentations, it can be worth the premium. Power delivery is another hidden gotcha. Some portable monitors can pass through power to charge your laptop even while using the monitor. That’s great for reducing cable clutter, but you need a monitor that supports at least 60W or 65W power delivery, otherwise your laptop might not charge at full speed. Check both your monitor’s and laptop’s power needs. A cheap monitor may only deliver 15W, which won’t charge a modern ultrabook under load.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my laptop supports USB-C video output?

Look for a DisplayPort (DP) logo next to the USB-C port, or check your laptop model’s specifications for ‘DisplayPort Alt Mode’ or ‘Thunderbolt 3/4.’ If none of your USB-C ports support video, you’ll need an adapter with separate power.

Can I use a portable monitor with a phone?

Yes, many recent Android phones support video output over USB-C, and iPhones require a Lightning-to-HDMI adapter. However, the phone’s battery drains quickly, and not all apps scale well to the larger screen.

Is 4K resolution worth it on a 15-inch portable monitor?

For most people, no. Text and images look very sharp at 1080p on a 15-inch screen. 4K offers diminishing returns at that size and demands more from your laptop’s graphics and battery. It’s only worth it for professional photo or video editing.

Do I need a separate power cable for the portable monitor?

If your laptop’s USB-C port supports both video and power delivery, you can run the monitor from a single cable. If not, you’ll need to plug the monitor into its own power source (usually via a second USB-C cable to a wall adapter).

Are portable monitors good for gaming?

They can be, but look for models with 120 Hz or higher refresh rates and low response times. Most portable monitors are 60 Hz and fine for casual games. Fast-paced shooters benefit from higher refresh, but expect higher prices and more battery drain.

Will a portable monitor work with a MacBook?

Yes, but MacBooks with M1, M2, or M3 chips natively support only one external display (some M-series Max/Ultra chips support more). Check your specific model before buying. You may need a special adapter or a monitor that uses DisplayLink technology for multi-monitor setups.

In shortStart with the three questions: does your USB-C port output video, will you work outdoors (brightness), and do you need touch? From there, pick a 15-inch IPS monitor with a solid integrated stand for the best all-around portable experience. The right choice balances weight, brightness, and connection type, don’t overpay for features you won’t actually use on the road.
